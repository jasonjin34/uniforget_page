Abstract

Recent advances in generative models have demonstrated an exceptional ability to produce highly realistic images. However, previous studies show that generated images often resemble the training data, and this problem becomes more severe as the model size increases. Memorizing training data can lead to legal challenges, including copyright infringement, violations of portrait rights, and trademark violations. Existing approaches to mitigating memorization mainly focus on manipulating the denoising sampling process to steer image embeddings away from the memorized embedding space, or employ unlearning methods that require training on datasets containing specific sets of memorized concepts. However, existing methods often incur substantial computational overhead during sampling, or focus narrowly on removing one or more groups of target concepts, imposing a significant limitation on their scalability.

To understand and mitigate these problems, our work, UniForget, offers a new perspective on understanding the root cause of memorization. Our work demonstrates that specific parts of the model are responsible for copyrighted content generation. By applying model pruning, we can effectively suppress the probability of generating copyrighted content without targeting specific concepts, while preserving the general generative capabilities of the model. Additionally, we show that our approach is both orthogonal and complementary to existing unlearning methods, thereby highlighting its potential to improve current unlearning and de-memorization techniques.

Two hypotheses

Memorized and neutral embeddings come apart

The latent embeddings of memorized content can be separated from the embedding space of de-memorized features. Modifying or removing the memorized embeddings does not significantly affect the generation of neutral features.

A small set of parameters carries the memory

A small subset of model parameters influences the reproduction of copyrighted content. These parameters push generated features away from the intended neutral embedding space — so removing them is enough.

Method

A learnable mask \mathcal{M} is applied to the feedforward, attention and normalization layers of the flow-matching backbone, relaxed to a differentiable form during training. Training uses 256 neutral prompts drawn from LAION-2B and filtered by GPT-4o — no copyrighted prompt is ever shown to the optimizer:

The first term is a reconstruction loss between the denoised latent of the original model and that of the masked model, which preserves generative ability on neutral prompts. The second term counts how much of the model stays switched on. The mask is relaxed with

Nothing in this objective mentions a concept, a character, or a celebrity. The only lever is \beta: raise it and the optimizer switches off more of the model to satisfy the sparsity term, which is why one hyperparameter turns into a de-memorization dial.

Orthogonal to concept unlearning

UniForget removes what it was never told about; concept-erasure methods remove exactly what they are told about. The two do not compete. UniForget treats "nudity" as neutral and leaves it alone — but composed with Concept Ablation, both problems are handled at once, and quality degrades far less than concept erasure alone as the number of targets grows.

Which layers hold the memory

Masking attention alone barely helps and wrecks quality. Feedforward and normalization layers carry the memorization — masking those two gives the best de-memorization at essentially unchanged FID.

Limitations

Strong de-memorization also forgets some neutral concepts. A short retraining phase recovers most of that loss, but the trade-off is real and worth stating plainly.

Evaluation depends on VLM judgment. GPT-4o and LLaVA 1.6 disagree with each other, sometimes flag copyright-free content, and are not stable judges of what infringement looks like — a difficulty they share with human annotators. Reliable detection of copyright-sensitive content remains open, and we treat it as future work alongside extending the method to SD3 and visual autoregressive models.
